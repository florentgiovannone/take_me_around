The Age of Bronze by Auguste Rodin, French, 1840 to 1917.

A life-size male nude, one hand on his head, the other raised — Rodin's first independent masterpiece, so disconcertingly real that the Paris Salon accused him of casting it directly from the body.

About the work.

A life-size bronze man stands with his right arm raised high above his head and his left forearm held near his shoulder. His head falls back; eyes are closed or lifted toward the ceiling. Weathered green-black patina covers the chest, ribs, and abdomen, catching light along the collarbones and the raised arm. The pose is alert yet suspended — as if waking, or remembering.

Rodin began the figure in October 1875 in Brussels, after returning from Italy and his first encounter with Michelangelo. His model was Auguste Neyt, a young Belgian soldier. Neyt later remembered standing for hours while Rodin shifted the pose by inches.

Originally the figure held a long spear in his left hand, and Rodin titled him Le Vaincu — The Vanquished — in memory of the defeated French. Then he removed the spear. With the weapon gone, the gesture became unreadable: is the man waking up, dying, dreaming, lifting himself out of nothing? Rodin renamed him The Age of Bronze after Hesiod's Third Age of Man, and the ambiguity stuck. He stands halfway between sleep and consciousness, halfway between idealised classical nude and something startlingly modern.

When the plaster reached the Salons of Brussels and Paris in 1877, critics accused Rodin of casting from life. A group of his peers — Falguière, Carrier-Belleuse, Dubois — finally signed a public defence. The State bought the plaster and commissioned this, the first bronze, from Thiébaut Frères in 1880. Rodin was awarded a third-class medal at the Salon. He never made a life-size figure again. He had already learned what he needed.
