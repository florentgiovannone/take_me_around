The Secret by Auguste Rodin, French, 1840 to 1917.

About the work.

A man leans forward from the left, his back arched and one hand planted on the craggy base. A woman sits against the rock to his right, head lifted to meet him, her arm reaching around his neck to draw him down. Their lips touch at the centre of the group. Rodin leaves the faces slightly softened — less portrait than gesture — but the bodies read clearly: shoulder, spine, thigh, the weight of one figure settling into the other. The bronze carries a dark brown patina with green in the hollows of the rock and along the spine. Light slides across the polished ridges of muscle and catches in the tool-marked skin.

Rodin conceived the group around 1894. This cast was made in 1960 at the Georges Rudier foundry in Paris under licence from the Musée Rodin — signed, stamped with the founder's mark, and marked with the museum copyright on the base. At twenty-seven point nine centimetres it is deliberately small: an embrace you approach at close range rather than from across a gallery.

Walk around it slowly. From behind you see the man's strained back and the woman's curved spine; from the front, the closed circuit of their arms; from the side, the narrow space where the kiss actually happens. The title asks you to read privacy into what is, on the surface, an open display of tenderness.
