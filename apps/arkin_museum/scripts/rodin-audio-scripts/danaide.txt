About the work.

She is folded into herself on a craggy base: knees drawn under, hips raised, face hidden against her arm. Long hair spills down the rock face in thick bronze strands that merge with the texture of the stone beneath her. The back and shoulders catch the light; the hollows along the spine and beneath the folded limbs hold shadow and green patina. There is no jar, no other figure — only this compact, muscular body given over to weight and exhaustion. Rodin's signature sits on the side of the base.

In Greek myth the Danaïdes were condemned to draw water forever in vessels that could not hold it. Rodin modelled one of them around 1885 for The Gates of Hell, then let her stand alone, as he did with so many figures lifted out of that monumental swarm. This Arkın cast is bronze with a rich dark brown and red-brown patination — not the famous white marble in Paris, but the same pose preserved at twenty-one centimetres.

Move around the base. From above you read the arc of the spine; from the side, the raised curve of the hip and the pour of hair; from behind, the shoulders braced as if still carrying something you cannot see. The punishment lives in the posture, not in any attribute Rodin chose to leave out.
