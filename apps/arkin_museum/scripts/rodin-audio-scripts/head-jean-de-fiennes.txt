Head of Jean de Fiennes by Auguste Rodin, French, 1840 to 1917.

A bronze head thrown sharply backward — mouth open, eyes sunk in shadow, anguish concentrated in a face without a body.

About the work.

Only the head survives, mounted on a two-tier black pedestal. It tilts back and slightly to one side, mouth parted, eyes hollowed into darkness. The modelling is rough and urgent — hair in thick clumps, skin lumpy with tool marks, highlights catching on the forehead and cheekbone while the sockets stay black. There is no torso, no drapery, no outstretched arms — just this isolated face at thirty-two point two centimetres.

Jean de Fiennes was the youngest of the six burghers of Calais who offered themselves as hostages in 1347. Rodin received the monument commission in 1884 and modelled each citizen as a separate psychological study. This head was conceived in 1886; the Arkın cast was made in 1984 at the Émile Godard foundry under Musée Rodin licence.

Stand low and the backward throw reads as defiance; stand high and it reads as surrender. Rodin gives you a single angle of a young man's fear and lets the rest go unstated.
